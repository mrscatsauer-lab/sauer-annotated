Ask a room full of teenagers for the main idea of a nonfiction passage and you'll usually get one of three answers. The topic ("it's about the Dust Bowl"). A random detail from the last paragraph. Or a long, faithful retelling of everything in order, which is technically correct and completely useless.

That last one has a name. Researchers call it **knowledge telling**: repeating the text instead of understanding how its ideas fit together. What we want is **knowledge transformation**, where a student can explain how the ideas connect and why the author put them in that order.

The KAT framework is built to get kids from one to the other.

## What KAT is

KAT stands for Knowledge Acquisition and Transformation. It was developed by Kay Wijekumar and her colleagues at Texas A&M, and it used to go by a much longer name (the Framework for the Accelerated Strategic Comprehension of Text, which you can see why they changed).

<aside class="note">full disclosure: this came out of my own university. gig 'em.</aside>

The big idea is simple. Good writers organize information on purpose, using a handful of structures. If students can recognize the structure, they know what the important ideas are and how they connect. The structure becomes a map.

KAT focuses on four main structures:

- **Comparison:** how two or more things are alike or different
- **Problem and solution:** something went wrong, and here's what was done about it
- **Cause and effect:** one thing led to another
- **Sequence:** steps or events in order

(There's also description, but it usually lives inside one of the others.)

Then students follow the same routine every time: identify the structure, write a main idea using a sentence stem that matches it, build a summary from that main idea, and use all of it to make inferences and answer questions.

## Before reading: preteach the words

A structure won't help a student who can't get through the sentences. So before anyone reads, I'd do two kinds of vocabulary work.

**The content words.** Pick a few words that students need to understand this passage and will see again in other texts. Not every hard word. Just the ones that would sink them. Give a student-friendly definition, use it in a sentence that sounds like real life, and have them use it once themselves. Beck, McKeown, and Kucan's *Bringing Words to Life* is the classic guide for choosing and teaching these words.

**The signal words.** This is the part people skip. Words like *however*, *as a result*, *because*, *on the other hand*, and *the solution was* are the clues that tell you the structure. If a student doesn't notice them, the whole text reads like a flat list of facts. Teach them directly, the same way you'd teach any vocabulary word.

## During reading: find the structure

As students read, they hunt for signal words and decide which structure the author used for the whole passage, not just one sentence. Here's a short example:

> In the 1930s, farmers on the Great Plains had plowed up millions of acres of native grass. When a long drought hit, there was nothing left to hold the soil in place. **As a result**, strong winds lifted the dry dirt into enormous dust storms. **Because** the land could no longer grow crops, thousands of families lost their farms and moved west.

The signal words (*when*, *as a result*, *because*) point to **cause and effect**. That one decision tells students what to look for: what caused what.

## After reading: build the main idea

Now the main idea isn't a guess. Students use a sentence stem that matches the structure:

> Plowing up the grassland and a long drought **caused** massive dust storms, **which caused** thousands of families to lose their farms.

From there, the summary is just the main idea plus the most important supporting details. And inference questions get easier, because students can see the chain. (Why didn't the farmers just plant again the next year? Look at the cause.)

<aside class="note">the stem does the heavy lifting. kids stop staring at a blank line.</aside>

## Where comprehension strategies fit

KAT isn't a replacement for the comprehension strategies you already teach. It gives them something to stand on. Main idea, summarizing, and inferring are all built right into the routine, and once students have the structure, things like author's purpose make more sense too. An author who writes in problem-and-solution is usually trying to convince you the solution works.

## An honest note for high school teachers

Most of the KAT research has been done in grades 4 through 8, and it's strongest with informational text. Studies of the web-based version found that students, including struggling readers and English learners, outperformed comparison groups on reading comprehension. Texas A&M is now scaling it up in middle schools across several states.

I haven't seen published studies with high school students yet, so using it in grades 9–12 is an extension, not a proven fact. But the problem it solves doesn't disappear at ninth grade. If you teach high school, you've met students who still read nonfiction as one long list of facts. A clear routine for finding the structure is useful at any age, especially for the students who are still catching up.

It also works best when it's routine. Short and frequent beats one big unit. Try it with the nonfiction you're already teaching: an article paired with a novel, a historical document, a speech.
