Everyone calls *Romeo and Juliet* the greatest love story ever told. That's generous. <mark>It's the greatest story ever told about two teenagers who could not wait five minutes.</mark>

And it isn't even original. Shakespeare was working off a story that was already about 1,600 years old, from the Roman poet Ovid. Same forbidden love. Same secret meeting. Same tragic misunderstanding. The main difference is that Ovid's version has a lion.

## Meet the original

In Ovid's *Metamorphoses*, Pyramus and Thisbe are next-door neighbors in Babylon. They fall in love, their parents say absolutely not, and so the two of them do what any reasonable couple would do: they find a crack in the wall between their houses and whisper sweet nothings through it. Every night they kiss the wall, each on their own side, which means the wall gets more action than anyone else in this story.

Eventually they plan to sneak out and meet under a mulberry tree by a tomb outside the city. Thisbe gets there first. A lioness wanders up to drink from a nearby spring, her mouth still bloody from an earlier meal. Thisbe runs and hides, and on the way she drops her veil. The lioness, finished with her drink, gives the veil a chew and leaves.

<aside class="note">she wasn't even hungry. she was just thirsty. the lion is the only one in this story with a reasonable plan.</aside>

Then Pyramus shows up. He sees lion tracks. He sees a bloody veil. He does not look around. He does not call her name. He immediately concludes that Thisbe is dead, decides it's his fault, and falls on his sword.

Ovid, a poet, describes the blood spraying up like water from a burst pipe. It splashes the white mulberries and turns them dark red. Thisbe comes back, finds him, and uses the same sword on herself. The gods are moved, and mulberries stay dark forever in their memory.

## Same song, new arrangement

Put them side by side and Shakespeare isn't exactly hiding it. Disapproving parents become a full-blown family feud. The wall becomes a balcony, so there's still a building between them and they're still whispering in the dark. Ovid's climax happens at a tomb, and Shakespeare's happens in one. A bloody veil becomes a girl who only looks dead. And both times, a guy dies because he jumped to a conclusion, and the girl shows up just in time to be too late.

## A brief timeline of Romeo's week

The thing nobody remembers from ninth grade is how fast this all happens. The whole play takes about four days.

- **Sunday morning:** Romeo is sobbing over a girl named Rosaline. He will love her forever.
- **Sunday night:** Romeo has forgotten Rosaline exists.
- **Monday afternoon:** Romeo is married.
- **About an hour later:** Romeo kills his new wife's cousin and gets banished.
- **Wednesday:** Romeo hears Juliet is dead, buys poison the same day, rides back to Verona, kills a guy outside the tomb, and drinks the poison minutes before she wakes up.

<aside class="note">i have had leftovers in my fridge longer than this relationship.</aside>

And Juliet *knows*. On the night they meet, standing on that balcony, she says it out loud:

> It is too rash, too unadvised, too sudden; too like the lightning, which doth cease to be ere one can say "It lightens."

Then she marries him the next afternoon.

<aside class="note">self-awareness: 10. follow-through: 0.</aside>

## The adults are not helping

Friar Laurence gets the best advice in the whole play:

> Wisely and slow. They stumble that run fast.

Great advice. Then this same man secretly marries two kids he met that week, hands a thirteen-year-old a potion that makes her look dead, and sends the explanation to Romeo by mail. The mail gets stuck in a plague quarantine.

<aside class="note">his backup plan was the postal service.</aside>

Every year, someone asks why Juliet didn't just text him, and every year I don't have a good answer. The whole tragedy is basically a delivery problem.

## Shakespeare knew it was ridiculous

My favorite fact about this pairing: around the same time Shakespeare wrote *Romeo and Juliet*, he also wrote *A Midsummer Night's Dream*, and in that play a group of amateur actors performs Pyramus and Thisbe. Badly. On purpose.

A guy plays the Wall. He holds up his fingers to make the crack. Another guy plays the Lion and pauses to reassure the audience that he is not, in fact, a real lion. Pyramus delivers a heartfelt speech directly to the masonry. The audience onstage laughs the whole way through, and one of them sums it up perfectly:

> This is the silliest stuff that ever I heard.

Same plot, two genres. Whether it's a tragedy or a comedy depends on whether you take the lovers seriously, and Shakespeare clearly knew that from the outside, teenage love looks like the end of the world and a little bit of a farce at the same time.

## So what's it about?

Love, sure. The feelings are real, and Shakespeare never mocks them. Juliet's lines are some of the most gorgeous ever written about falling hard for someone.

But both stories are really about speed. Nobody in either one dies because they loved too much. They die because they decided too fast. Pyramus doesn't look for Thisbe. Romeo doesn't wait for anyone to explain. In both cases, five more minutes would have turned a tragedy into a really good story they told at their wedding.

---

## The verdict

Four stars. I'd still call it required reading, and it's better with Ovid next to it. Read Pyramus and Thisbe first, since it's only a few pages, and let your students (or your kids) figure out they already know how *Romeo and Juliet* ends.

Fair warning: it might backfire. When I taught them together, my students liked Pyramus and Thisbe better. Their official review of *Romeo and Juliet*, the most famous love story in the English language, was "mid."

<aside class="note">four hundred years of critics, and none of them were this brutal.</aside>

I can't totally argue with them. Ovid tells the whole thing in a few pages and includes a lion. Shakespeare takes five acts and spends most of Act 1 letting Romeo mope about Rosaline, a girl who never even shows up onstage. That's where my missing star went.
