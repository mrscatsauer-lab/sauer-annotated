Welcome to **What to Say When…**, short scripts for the moments parents freeze. Today's moment is a hard one: you're sitting in a small chair at a small table, and someone from your child's school says the words, "We think your child might need to repeat the grade."

I've been on both sides of that table. As a teacher, I've been close to a lot of these conversations. As a mom, we decided my daughter would repeat kindergarten, because it was what was best for her.

<aside class="note">i've never regretted it. but i asked a lot of questions first.</aside>

Here's what I know now: <mark>the extra year isn't what helps. The help is what helps.</mark> Research on retention keeps finding the same thing. Kids who repeat a grade without real support usually don't come out ahead. Kids who get early, specific, consistent help often do, whether they repeat or not.

So your job in that meeting isn't to decide "yes" or "no" on the spot. It's to find out what the help actually looks like.

## What not to say

- "Okay, whatever you think is best." (You know your kid. You're part of this decision.)
- "Absolutely not. They'll be embarrassed." (Maybe. But that's a feeling, not a plan. Hear them out first.)
- "So they'll just do the same thing again?" (Hold onto this one. It's actually the right question, just asked as an accusation. Ask it as a question instead. It's below.)

## What to ask instead

**To understand the problem:** "What specific skills is my child missing? Can you show me the work or the data?"

**To find out what's already been tried:** "What support has my child gotten this year, how often, and what changed because of it?"

**To see the plan:** "If my child moves up, what help will they get next year? Who will give it, and how often?"

**To test the alternative:** "If my child repeats the grade, what will be different about next year? Same teacher? Same lessons? Or a real plan?"

**To know if it's working:** "How will we know, and when, if the plan is working? When do we meet again?"

**To make it real:** "Can I get that plan in writing?"

<aside class="note">if nobody can answer the "who and how often" questions, that's your answer about whether there's a plan.</aside>

## What to say to your kid, either way

Kids hear "held back" and think "failed." Whatever you decide, the words you use at home matter.

**If they repeat the grade:** "You're not behind. You're getting the time your brain needs, and that's what smart people do."

**If they move up with extra help:** "You're moving up, and you've got a team helping you. Getting help isn't a bad thing. It's how everybody gets better."

**Either way:** "This isn't about how smart you are. It's about giving you what you need. And I'm on your team the whole time."

## One thing to try this week

If you're heading into one of these meetings, write your questions on an index card and bring it with you. Small tables and serious faces make everyone forget what they meant to ask.
