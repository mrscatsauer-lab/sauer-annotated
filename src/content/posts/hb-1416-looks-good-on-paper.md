In my [last post](/posts/holding-kids-back/), I said Texas is moving in the right direction on grade retention. Instead of holding kids back after a failed STAAR test, the state now requires schools to help them catch up.

On paper, I love it. In practice, I've watched it turn into something else.

<aside class="note">this is the "on paper" post i promised myself i'd write.</aside>

## What the law says

House Bill 1416, passed in 2023, updated the accelerated instruction rules Texas created in 2021. Here's the short version:

- Students who don't reach "Approaches Grade Level" on STAAR get **accelerated instruction**: at least **15 hours** of tutoring, or **30 hours** for the students who scored lowest.
- Tutoring groups can have up to **four students per tutor**. Under the old law, it was three.
- The tutor has to be **trained in the instructional materials** and has to be the **same person** for the whole stretch.
- The old **accelerated learning committees**, the meetings where a team made a plan for each kid, are gone. Now a formal plan is only required after a student fails the same test **two years in a row**.

Small groups, consistent adults, and targeted help are exactly what the research on tutoring recommends. So what's the problem?

## Problem 1: Software counts as a tutor

Schools can skip the four-to-one rule if they use an approved computer program instead. For the 2026 to 2027 school year, TEA's [ratio waiver list](https://tea.texas.gov/taa-letters/hb-1416-ratio-waiver-list-2026-27-school-year) includes six products, including IXL, Zearn, and Read 180 Flex.

I'm not anti-technology. Some of these programs are useful for practice. But a program isn't a person.

<mark>A kid who can't read doesn't need headphones. They need someone sitting next to them who knows why they can't read.</mark>

When a struggling reader gets stuck, the program gives them the next question. A good teacher notices they're guessing from the first letter, or skipping lines, or shutting down the second a word has more than two syllables. Then they change what they're doing. Software can't hear a kid sigh.

<aside class="note">"he logged 15 hours" and "he learned something" are two different sentences.</aside>

## Problem 2: The wrong person in the chair

Even when there's a human in the room, it's often whoever has a free period.

I've seen students sent to teachers who are wonderful at their jobs but don't have the training to fix the problem in front of them. A reading gap in ninth grade isn't something you solve by having a kid reread the passage and answer more questions. It might be a decoding problem. It might be fluency. It might be vocabulary, or background knowledge, or stamina. Figuring out which one takes expertise.

"Trained in the instructional materials" means someone knows how to use the program. It doesn't mean they know how to teach reading.

The research backs this up. A large [review of tutoring studies](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27476) found that tutoring works best when it's delivered by teachers and trained paraprofessionals, during the school day, and consistently. Volunteers and untrained tutors get weaker results. In other words, who does the tutoring matters as much as whether it happens.

> Tutoring isn't magic. Expertise is.

I'm a certified reading specialist. I say that not to brag, but because most campuses have very few people with that kind of training, and they're rarely the ones assigned to the tutoring block.

## Problem 3: Hours, not help

Here's the part that frustrates me most. Too often, accelerated instruction becomes a compliance task.

The hours get scheduled. The attendance gets logged. The spreadsheet gets turned in. And nobody stops to ask the only question that matters: did it work?

Without the old committees, there's often no one whose job is to look at a kid's data and say, "This isn't helping. Let's try something else." A student can rack up their required hours, fail again, and start the same cycle the next year.

<aside class="note">a checkbox has never taught anyone to read.</aside>

There's also the money. The law didn't come with a big new pot of funding. Many districts paid for tutoring with federal pandemic relief money, and that ran out in 2024. So schools are stuck stretching the same staff even thinner, which pushes them right back toward software and free periods.

---

## What would actually make it work

I don't want to repeal HB 1416. The idea behind it is right. I want it to do what it promises.

- **Put the right person in the chair.** Kids with reading gaps should be tutored by teachers with reading expertise. Fund more reading specialists and interventionists, and protect their time for this.
- **Treat software as a supplement, not a substitute.** Programs can give practice. They shouldn't count as the tutor.
- **Measure progress, not hours.** Every few weeks, someone should look at whether the student is improving and change the plan if they aren't.
- **Bring back a team.** A student shouldn't have to fail twice before anyone sits down and makes a real plan.
- **Pay for it.** If the state requires high-impact tutoring, it should fund high-impact tutoring.

In my [post about PLCs](/posts/a-meeting-is-not-a-mentor/), I wrote about the coach who got me through my first two years. She didn't hand me a program. She sat with me, watched what I did, and told me the truth.

That's what struggling kids need, too: a person who knows what they're doing, every time, until it works.

<mark>Help that's only real on paper isn't help. It's paperwork.</mark>
