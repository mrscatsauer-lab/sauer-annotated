Fun fact: in the 1950 edition of *The Martian Chronicles*, Ray Bradbury set this story in August 2026. That's this year. We made it. Mostly.

<aside class="note">later editions moved it to 2057. coward.</aside>

The main character is a house. A smart home in California wakes the family, cooks breakfast, reminds everyone about the bills, and cleans up after them. There's just one problem: <mark>the family is gone.</mark> A nuclear blast has burned their silhouettes onto the outside wall. The house keeps going anyway, all day, until a fire finally takes it down.

My students loved it. A house that keeps running after everyone's gone hits different when you grew up talking to a smart speaker.

## The literary elements doing the heavy lifting

- **Personification:** the house panics, sings, and fights for its life. By the end, it's the most human thing in the story.
- **Imagery:** four white silhouettes on a scorched wall: a man mowing, a woman picking flowers, two kids with a ball. Two sentences, and you never forget them.
- **Situational irony:** a house built to protect a family outlives them. Technology does everything right except the one thing that mattered.
- **Allusion:** the title comes from a real poem by Sara Teasdale, written during World War I. The house reads it aloud to an empty room.
- **Symbolism:** the clock. Every section starts with the time, and the story ends when time stops meaning anything.
- **Mood:** cheerful voices, empty rooms. That gap is where the dread lives.

<aside class="note">the dog. we don't talk about the dog.</aside>

The poem is in the public domain, so here's the ending the house reads to nobody:

> And Spring herself, when she woke at dawn / Would scarcely know that we were gone.

<mark>It's a story about the end of the world where the world turns out to be fine. We're the only part that's missing.</mark>

## The verdict

Four and a half stars. Half a star off because of the dog. You know what you did, Ray.

**Read it if:** you teach personification, irony, or theme, or you've ever wondered what your smart speaker would do if you never came home.

**Skip it if:** you need a happy ending, or a dog who makes it.

**Pair it with:** Teasdale's full poem (just twelve lines), and the [Omelas lesson](/posts/omelas-fishbowl-lesson/) for another perfect world with a terrible price.
