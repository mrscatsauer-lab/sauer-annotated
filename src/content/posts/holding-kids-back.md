Every spring, the same conversation happens on campuses all over Texas.

A student has struggled all year. The data isn't where anyone hoped. And now somebody has to decide: move them up with their friends, or hold them back?

I've been close enough to a lot of those conversations to know how heavy they are. I've also been on the other side of the table. When my daughter was in kindergarten, we decided she would repeat it. It was what was best for her, and I've never regretted it.

<aside class="note">spoiler: this post does not end with me apologizing to her.</aside>

So for years, my opinion was firmly planted: retention works. Then I spent a semester of my doctoral program studying how the rest of the world handles this question.

The rest of the world mostly disagrees with me.

## The world is split, and the best systems hold back the fewest kids

Here's what surprised me most. There's no global agreement that struggling students should repeat a year, and the countries that hold back the most kids don't perform the best.

- **France** used to be one of the heaviest users of grade retention in the developed world. Repeating a year was routine, and it was estimated to cost the country about two billion euros a year. In 2014, France decided retention should happen only in exceptional cases, with extra support during the school year instead. By 2023, only about 1 percent of French students were repeating a grade, well under the international average.
- **Finland** treats retention as a true last resort. Students aren't tracked or sorted, and kids who struggle get support inside their regular classrooms, early, without the stigma.
- **Germany** goes the other way: about one in five students repeats a grade at some point.
- **The United States** sits in between. The most recent national count I could find, from 2016, found that about 7 percent of American kids ages 6 to 17 had repeated at least one grade. That's around 3.4 million kids.

<aside class="note">two billion euros. for the same year, twice.</aside>

So holding kids back isn't a law of nature. It's a tradition, and different countries have different traditions.

## The Florida twist

Here's where it gets interesting. Some of the strongest evidence *for* retention comes from right here in the U.S.

Starting in 2002, Florida required third graders who couldn't read at a basic level to repeat third grade. Most people expected that to backfire. Instead, researchers found that the retained kids did better in reading a year later than similar kids who were promoted.

But look closer. When researchers followed those students into high school, the advantage faded compared to kids their own age. Retention had no effect on whether they graduated. And Florida's retained kids didn't just sit through third grade again. They got intensive reading support.

<mark>When retention helped, it looks like the help is what did the work, not the repeated year.</mark>

That one stuck with me. I used to think the extra year was the magic. Now I think the question was always what happens *during* that year.

---

## Texas is moving in the right direction, on paper

Texas has started moving the same way as France and Finland.

For years, Texas tied promotion in certain grades to passing the STAAR test. In 2021, House Bill 4545 ended test-based retention in grades 5 and 8. Instead of holding kids back, schools now have to give students who fall short accelerated instruction. In 2023, House Bill 1416 sharpened that into high-impact tutoring: small groups, delivered by a consistent, trained educator. And starting in 2027 to 2028, STAAR itself is being replaced by shorter tests designed to give teachers and parents faster feedback.

Catch kids early. Support them as they move forward. That's exactly what the research says to do.

So why am I still worried?

## Social promotion isn't support either

Because I've seen students who couldn't read in middle school get passed on anyway.

Here's what the Texas Education Agency's own numbers look like for the 2023 to 2024 school year:

- **Kindergarten:** 1.6 percent of students retained
- **First grade:** 2.1 percent
- **Grades 3 through 8:** between 0.3 and 0.6 percent
- **Ninth grade:** 7.8 percent. That's 35,317 freshmen.

<aside class="note">0.3 percent in fifth grade. 7.8 percent in ninth. something happens in between, and it isn't a miracle.</aside>

To be fair, it's not a perfect comparison. High school retention is mostly about failing courses and missing credits, not a teacher's judgment call in May. But the pattern is hard to ignore. We almost never hold kids back in middle school. Then they hit high school, where the bill comes due all at once.

I teach high school English. I see where that road ends.

> Moving a kid forward without help isn't kindness. It's postponing the conversation until it's harder to have.

Requiring support is great in theory. But if the tutoring never shows up, or shows up as a checkbox on a form somebody has to turn in to the district, then we haven't replaced retention with anything. We've just stopped measuring the problem.

---

## So what do I actually believe now?

I'm not arguing that Texas should become Finland. You can't lift one country's system and drop it into another and expect it to work. But the research gave me a clearer picture of what to fight for:

- **Retention should be a genuine last resort,** not the default consequence of a low score.
- **Intervention should come first, and early.** That's the lesson from both France and Finland.
- **Support has to be real.** A specific plan, a specific person, a specific time, and a way to know whether it's working.
- **Teachers and parents should push back in the spring,** and ask what the help actually looks like before anyone says "promote" or "retain."

And as for my daughter? Holding her back was still the right choice for her. But I understand now that the year itself was never the point. What matters is whether a kid who's struggling gets help early, consistently, and in a way that's built for them.

<mark>The real question was never "promote or retain?" It's "who is helping this kid, and when?"</mark>
