Let me say this first so nobody gets confused: I am not here to defend STAAR.

Everybody hates it. Kids hate it. Teachers hate it. Parents hate it. If you want to throw a retirement party for that test, I'll bring a cake.

But I've spent the last few weeks digging through Texas reading policy for my doctoral research, and I keep landing on the same question. When STAAR leaves, what checks are left to make sure we aren't leaving kids behind?

Right now, I can't find a good answer.

## What's actually changing

In September 2025, Texas passed House Bill 8. Starting in the 2027–28 school year, STAAR is replaced by something called the Student Success Tool. Instead of one big test in the spring, students in grades 3 through 8 take three shorter ones: beginning of year, middle of year, and end of year. Scores are supposed to come back in two business days instead of weeks.

Parts of that sound good. Faster results are useful. A test in the fall could tell me something while I can still do something about it.

The high school part is what worries me. In high school, the beginning- and middle-of-year tests are optional for English I. And the English II end-of-course exam is gone completely, including as a graduation requirement.

<aside class="note">i teach English II. so that's my test leaving.</aside>

That means English I becomes the last state reading test most Texas students will ever take. After that, the state stops looking.

## Meanwhile, reading scores are falling

This isn't happening in a good year for reading.

On the 2024 Nation's Report Card, Texas eighth graders posted their lowest reading average in decades, and nearly 40% scored below the basic level. Nationally, high school seniors had the lowest reading scores the test has ever recorded. About a third of them scored below basic. In 1992, it was about one in five.

So reading is getting worse, and the state is checking less. Those two things should not be happening at the same time.

## The checks were already thin

Even with STAAR, Texas mostly stops paying attention to reading somewhere around middle school.

The state's big investment in the science of reading, the Reading Academies, is for kindergarten through third grade teachers. The Science of Teaching Reading certification exam covers teachers through eighth grade. A high school English teacher in Texas doesn't need either one.

That matters, because by high school, a student who's behind in reading might be behind for completely different reasons. One kid can't decode longer words. Another reads every word correctly but so slowly that the meaning falls apart. Another reads fluently and still can't tell you what happened. Those three kids need three different kinds of help.

STAAR could tell me a student was behind. It never told me why. Figuring out the why was always left to the teacher, and most high school teachers were never trained to do it. Research backs this up. Secondary teachers often don't feel qualified, or even responsible, for teaching reading (Ness, 2009). And a 2026 meta-analysis found that teachers' knowledge of how reading works is limited and doesn't improve just from years in the classroom (Georgiou et al., 2026).

<aside class="note">experience doesn't fix it. training does. that one stung a little.</aside>

So the old system flagged kids and handed them to people who didn't know how to diagnose them. The new system flags fewer of them.

## Where do the kids in the gap go?

This is my real question.

Under [HB 1416](/posts/hb-1416-looks-good-on-paper/), a student who fails a state test gets accelerated instruction. I've seen what that looks like. Students who failed STAAR reading get put into "tutoring," which means a computer program that has them read grade-level texts and answer questions about them.

Think about that for a second. A kid who can't read grade-level text gets more grade-level text. Nobody checks whether the problem is decoding, fluency, vocabulary, or comprehension. Nothing is in place to help with the actual gap. The program just keeps asking the question they already couldn't answer.

<aside class="note">that's not tutoring. that's a retake with a mouse.</aside>

Still, at least there was a trigger. Something told the school, "This student needs help."

When the tests change, what pulls the alarm? If a district skips the optional English I fall test, nobody finds out a freshman is struggling until the spring. And if a sophomore can't read well, there's no state test left that will notice at all.

I'm not saying a test is the answer. A test is a smoke detector. It doesn't put out the fire. But you don't take the smoke detectors out of the building because they're annoying and then act surprised when nobody notices the smoke.

## What would actually help

We already know what works for older struggling readers. In one two-year study, ninth and tenth graders with reading difficulties made real gains when their intervention was taught by teachers who had been trained and coached (Vaughn et al., 2015). Not a program. Not a log. A trained person who knew what to look for.

So here's what I'd ask for:

If the state is going to stop testing reading after English I, then it needs to make sure someone on every high school campus can figure out why a student is struggling. Train high school English teachers in how reading works, not just how to teach novels. Fund reading specialists and give them time to work with kids. Give schools a simple, quick way to check reading in the fall, and make it more than optional. And when a student is behind, require a plan based on what's actually wrong, not just a number of hours.

Getting rid of a test everybody hates is easy. Making sure a sixteen-year-old can read is the hard part. I'd like someone in Austin to tell me who's doing the hard part.
