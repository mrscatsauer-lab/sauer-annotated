Teenagers are already experts in characterization. They just call it something else. Ask a fifteen-year-old to explain indirect characterization and you'll get a blank stare. Ask them whether "says all his exes were crazy" is a red flag and you'll get a ten-minute TED talk.

<mark>This lesson takes the skill they already have and makes them back it up with a line number.</mark>

<aside class="note">same skill. better vocabulary. fewer blank stares.</aside>

It fits in one class period (about 50 minutes) and works best once students have read at least through Act 3. Romeo and Juliet is perfect for it, because almost everyone in Verona has at least one flag that changes color depending on the scene.

## What you need

- Red and green sticky notes. Grab some yellow ones too, for beige flags (more on that in a second).
- One sheet of chart paper per group, labeled with a character's name.
- The Flag Hunt handout at the bottom of this post. Hit the print button and it lands on its own page.

---

## The lesson

### 1. Hook: Red, Green, or Beige? (5 minutes)

Give everyone a red card and a green card. Read a few real-life behaviors and have them vote:

- Texts back immediately.
- Says all his exes were crazy.
- Remembers your coffee order.
- Proposes after knowing you for one day.

Watch the room on that last one. Then tell them it's Romeo.

<aside class="note">let that land. let them sit in it.</aside>

Add a third category: **beige flags**. These are quirky, harmless traits that are neither good nor bad, like a guy who names his car or a girl who reads the last page of a book first. Students love arguing about what counts as beige, and it teaches them that not every trait is a judgment.

### 2. Mini-lesson: flags are characterization (5 minutes)

Here's the connection: a red or green flag is just indirect characterization with an opinion attached. Review STEAL, the five ways an author reveals character:

- **S**peech: what they say
- **T**houghts: what they think
- **E**ffect on others: how other characters react to them
- **A**ctions: what they do
- **L**ooks: how they appear

Then set the one rule for the day: <mark>no flag counts without a quote and a scene.</mark> "Romeo is giving red flag energy" is an opinion. "Romeo blames fate in 3.1 instead of taking responsibility" is evidence.

### 3. Flag Hunt (20 minutes)

Put each group on one character. Students hunt through the text and stick flags on their chart paper. Each note needs the quote, the scene, the STEAL category, and one sentence about what it reveals.

Here's a starter key for you, not for them. Let them find these on their own.

**Romeo**
- Red: in love with Rosaline in the morning, in love with Juliet by night (1.1 to 1.5).
- Green: he genuinely tries to make peace with Tybalt: "I do protest I never injured thee" (3.1).
- Red: after killing Tybalt, he cries, "O, I am fortune's fool!" Translation: not my fault. (3.1)

**Juliet**
- Green: "It is too rash, too unadvised, too sudden" (2.2). Self-awareness!
- Green: she tells him that if his "purpose" is marriage, he should send word tomorrow (2.2). That's a girl with boundaries.
- Red: she knows it's too fast, and does it anyway.

**Friar Laurence**
- Green: "Wisely and slow. They stumble that run fast" (2.3).
- Red: the secret wedding, the fake-death potion, the plan that depends on the mail.
- Very red: he hears the watch coming and leaves Juliet alone in the tomb: "I dare no longer stay" (5.3).

**The Nurse**
- Green: she loves Juliet like her own daughter, and she's funny about it.
- Red: after Romeo is banished, she tells Juliet to marry Paris and calls Romeo "a dishclout" compared to him (3.5).

<aside class="note">students take the nurse's betrayal personally. like, personally personally.</aside>

**Lord Capulet**
- Green: at the party, he stops Tybalt from starting a fight with Romeo (1.5).
- Red: when Juliet refuses to marry Paris, he tells her she can "hang, beg, starve, die in the streets" (3.5).

**Tybalt**
- Red: "What, drawn, and talk of peace? I hate the word" (1.1).
- Green: good luck. Let them try.

**Mercutio** and **Benvolio** make good bonus characters for bigger classes. Mercutio is mostly beige flags until 3.1, which is a discussion in itself.

### 4. Flag Court (12 minutes)

Each group presents its single strongest flag. Then court is in session. Any student can object, but only with counterevidence from the text. If the objection holds up, the flag gets overturned or changes color.

This is where the real thinking happens. Students discover that a lot of flags flip depending on the scene. Romeo's intensity is a green flag on the balcony and a red one in the street. Capulet is a reasonable host in Act 1 and a tyrant in Act 3. Same character, different evidence.

<aside class="note">appoint a judge with a gavel. a glue stick works. it's about the energy.</aside>

### 5. Exit ticket (5 minutes)

Pick one:

- Who in Verona has the most red flags? Prove it in three sentences with one quote.
- *Honors:* Name a flag that changes color over the course of the play. When does it change, and why?

---

## Honors extension

If you have time or want a second day, push them here: **flags depend on culture.**

To Shakespeare's audience, a daughter defying her father was a serious red flag. To a modern audience, Juliet refusing a forced marriage is a green one. Obedience, loyalty, and passion all mean different things depending on who's watching.

Seminar questions to try:

1. Who decides what counts as a red flag: the character, the audience, or the author?
2. Does Shakespeare want us to see Romeo and Juliet's love as a green flag or a red one? Find a line that supports your answer.
3. Which adult in this play has the most red flags? Are the adults more responsible for the ending than the teenagers?
4. Is any character in this play all green? If not, what does that say about Shakespeare's view of people?

> Every character is a mix of red and green. The question is which one wins when it counts.
