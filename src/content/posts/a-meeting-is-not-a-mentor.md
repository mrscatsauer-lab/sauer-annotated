On my first day as a teacher, I cried after second period.

Not a single dignified tear, either. I walked straight out of my classroom at a Title I school, found my instructional coach, and told her I had no idea what I'd gotten myself into.

Y'all, I'm not talking about my twenties. I'm talking about four years ago. I was 38.

<aside class="note">a grown woman, hiding in the coach's office. character development.</aside>

She talked me down. She told me to give it a week. Just one.

It turns out I love teaching. But I would not be the teacher I am today without her, and without the daily PLC she ran, for the first two years of my career.

## What that coach actually did

She didn't hand me a binder and wish me luck. She was there, every day, for two years. Here's what that looked like:

- **She was honest with me.** Not mean, not fake-nice. Honest. When something wasn't working, she said so, and then she helped me fix it.
- **She talked me down from anxiety.** More than once. New teachers carry a lot, and she knew the difference between a bad lesson and a bad career choice.
- **She recorded my classes** and watched them with me. Then she gave me real feedback. If you have never watched yourself teach on video, I can tell you it's humbling. It's also the fastest way to get better.
- **She made PLC about data.** Every day. I learned how to read student data, what it was actually telling me, and how to use it to plan the next lesson.

<mark>That last one changed everything. I didn't just learn to teach. I learned to look at evidence and adjust.</mark>

<aside class="note">i still do this every week. she just doesn't get the credit anymore. so: hi. thank you.</aside>

## Then I changed campuses

These days, I'm at a campus where PLC is few and far between. Most of us are lone rangers, the only teacher of our subject in the building. There's nobody down the hall teaching the same lesson I am.

And here's the honest part: I'm okay. Four years in, I don't need a meeting every day. I know how to read my data. I know when a lesson flopped and what to try next. I still cherish the time to sit with other teachers, listen, and steal their good ideas. I just don't need it daily anymore.

But I think about first-year me constantly. If she had landed on this campus, alone, with no coach and no PLC, she would have been a completely different teacher. Possibly not a teacher at all.

So here's my hill: **PLCs are great, but they need the right time, the right place, and a knowledgeable coach.** Without those three things, a PLC is just a meeting with a fancier name.

## What the research says

I spent part of my doctoral coursework reviewing a study on exactly this. Catheleen M. Benedict's 2014 dissertation at Rutgers followed a single fourth-grade team as it tried to turn a messy PLC into a productive one.

The starting point will sound familiar to every teacher reading this. The meetings had no real structure. They didn't always look at student data, and when they did, nobody connected the numbers to their own teaching. Conversations drifted away from curriculum and instruction.

<aside class="note">"drifted away from instruction" is academic for "we talked about the parking lot for twenty minutes."</aside>

So Benedict added structure, and four things happened:

1. **Trust came first.** Teachers didn't want to reflect openly at the start. Collaboration wasn't natural, and nobody knew what was expected. Trust had to be built on purpose.
2. **Protocols changed the conversation.** Once teachers had clear guidelines for discussing data, the off-topic chatter dried up. They started talking about student patterns and instructional concerns instead.
3. **Teachers started reflecting.** The deeper they dug into the data, the clearer their shared goals got. Collaboration became about improving instruction, not about filling the required meeting time.
4. **They found a real problem.** The team discovered a shared weakness: students struggled with higher-level vocabulary, synonyms, antonyms, and context. They didn't just notice low scores. They asked how their own teaching might have contributed.

<mark>That's the part that matters most to me: data alone doesn't improve instruction.</mark> Teachers can stare at numbers all day. The magic happens when someone guides the conversation from "what are the scores?" to "what are we doing that led to these scores?"

That someone is usually a coach.

## To be fair

It was one small team at one school, and the researcher was part of the group she was studying. The study also reported positive outcomes for students without showing measurable achievement data. So it's not proof that structured PLCs raise test scores everywhere.

> Scheduling a meeting is not the same thing as building a learning community.

But it lines up with every PLC I've been part of, good and bad. The structured one, with an administrator and a coach in the room, made me a better teacher. The unstructured ones mostly made me late for lunch.

## Time, place, and coach

Here's what I'd tell any principal building a PLC schedule.

**Time.** New teachers need PLC daily. Their first two years are survival plus a crash course in everything nobody taught them in college. Veteran teachers need it less often, but it should still be purposeful. A monthly meeting that matters beats a daily one that doesn't.

**Place.** A PLC needs structure: protocols for looking at data, clear expectations, and a culture where it's safe to say "my lesson bombed." Without trust, nobody reflects honestly. Without reflection, nothing changes.

**Coach.** Someone has to know what they're doing. Someone who can read the data, ask the hard question, watch a recorded lesson, and tell a crying 38-year-old to give it a week. A coach turns a meeting into a mentorship.

And for campuses full of lone rangers like mine? Get creative. Pair teachers across grade levels or across subjects. A monthly PLC built around shared literacy practices would work for almost every teacher in the building, because every subject is a reading subject. When teachers look at patterns together, they share what works, and struggling students benefit.

## Give it a week

I think a lot about how close I came to walking out after second period. One honest person, one week, and two years of daily support turned that into a career I love.

Every new teacher deserves that coach. Every school deserves a PLC that actually means something.

And if you're a brand-new teacher reading this after a rough second period: give it a week. Then go find your coach.
