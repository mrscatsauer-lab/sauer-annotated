Picture a scripted lesson. It tells the teacher exactly what to say. *Say: "Today we will identify the author's purpose."* Pause. Call on a student. Read the next line.

Now picture the moment every teacher knows is coming. A kid raises their hand and asks something that isn't in the script. Or the whole class stares blankly at the example. Or half of them already know this and the other half are three grade levels behind.

<mark>The script doesn't have a line for that. The teacher has to.</mark>

<aside class="note">this is every class period. every single one.</aside>

So here's my hill: **scripted curriculum is not better than teacher-written curriculum.** What schools are missing isn't a better script. It's better-trained teachers.

## I get the appeal. I really do.

Scripted programs aren't built by villains. They promise real things. Consistency from one classroom to the next. Lessons that line up with state standards. Support for brand-new teachers who are drowning. And time: if the lessons are already written, teachers can spend their evenings on something besides planning.

Texas is all in on this idea. During the 2025 to 2026 school year, [619 districts](https://tea.texas.gov/about-tea/newsroom/tea-releases-bluebonnet-learning-first-year-results) adopted at least one of the state's free, highly structured Bluebonnet Learning programs district-wide. That's a lot of classrooms reading from the same page.

And I understand why a principal facing a teacher shortage would look at a ready-made, state-approved curriculum and think, finally, one less thing.

## What the research actually says

I spent a semester digging into the research on scripted versus teacher-developed curriculum for my doctoral coursework. Here's what I found, minus the APA formatting.

**Scripts don't reliably beat good teaching.** A 2022 dissertation study of Tennessee elementary schools compared reading scores for students taught with scripted programs and students taught with traditional instruction. No significant difference. What *did* matter? Teacher knowledge, how well the lessons were taught, and whether the teacher adapted them for the kids in front of them.

<aside class="note">read that again. the curriculum wasn't the deciding factor. the teacher was.</aside>

**Teachers adapt scripts anyway.** When researchers [interviewed middle school reading teachers](https://doi.org/10.1177/0888406421992377) using a scripted program, they found the teachers changed it constantly. Not because they were rebellious, but because they had to. Following it exactly as written wasn't realistic with real students and real time limits. Which raises a funny problem: if nobody is teaching the script as written, what exactly are we measuring when we say the script works?

**Scripts change how teachers see their jobs.** A [study of teacher autonomy](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220272.2023.2297229) found that teachers working from scripted lessons felt less control over their own classrooms. The Tennessee study also found new teachers were afraid to step off the script, even when they could see it wasn't working.

<mark>That last one keeps me up at night. We're training new teachers to follow instead of think.</mark>

**And almost none of this research is about high school.** Most studies focus on elementary reading, where much of the work is decoding and phonics. Structured programs can help there. But by the time students hit my classroom, I'm teaching analysis, argument, and interpretation. You can't script a Socratic seminar. Believe me, I've tried to imagine it.

## To be fair to Texas

TEA's [first-year report](https://tea.texas.gov/about-tea/newsroom/tea-releases-bluebonnet-learning-first-year-results) says districts using Bluebonnet saw slightly bigger STAAR gains than districts that didn't. That's worth taking seriously.

It's also worth reading closely. Districts chose whether to adopt it, so the ones that signed up may have been different from the start. The report doesn't claim the curriculum caused the gains, and TEA says it's still figuring out what's driving them. I'm a reading specialist. Close reading is the whole job.

<aside class="note">correlation, causation. we teach this to sophomores.</aside>

## The real problem is training

A script can tell a teacher what to say. It can't teach them what to do when it doesn't work. That takes expertise, and expertise has to be built.

Think about every other profession we trust. We don't hand new doctors a script and send them into surgery. They do a residency, working alongside experienced physicians who watch, correct, and coach them for years. Electricians apprentice. Pilots log hours with an instructor in the seat beside them.

> A recipe doesn't make you a chef. Somebody has to teach you what to do when the sauce breaks.

Teachers need the same thing. Here's what I'd fund before I'd buy another script:

- **Deep content knowledge.** English teachers who actually know literature, writing, and how reading develops, not just how to deliver a lesson about it.
- **Real mentorship.** New teachers paired with expert teachers in their own field, with time built into the schedule to watch each other teach, plan together, and talk about what went wrong.
- **Ongoing coaching,** not a one-day training in August that everyone forgets by October.
- **Time to plan.** Good lessons don't appear by magic. They take hours. If we want teachers to write great curriculum, we have to give them the time to do it.

## Scaffold, not a cage

I'm not saying throw out every curriculum and make teachers build everything from scratch at 11 p.m. Good materials help. A strong curriculum in the hands of a trained teacher is a gift.

The difference is who's in charge. In the best classrooms, the curriculum is a scaffold. The teacher uses it, bends it, cuts what doesn't work, and adds what the kids need. In the worst ones, it's a cage, and the teacher is just reading the lines.

Students don't need someone who can read a script. They need someone who knows exactly what to do when the kid in the back row asks the question nobody wrote down.

That's a teacher. Let's start training them like it.
