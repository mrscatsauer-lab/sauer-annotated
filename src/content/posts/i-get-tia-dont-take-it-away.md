This week, the Texas Tribune asked Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa whether she supports the Teacher Incentive Allotment. Her answer:

> I'm not a big fan of the Teacher Incentive Allotment because it requires teachers to jump through more hoops to get more money, and teachers already do too much.

Asked if she'd transition away from it, she said, "I would want to transition away from it. Yes."

I receive TIA. So let me tell you what it is, what's wrong with it, and why getting rid of it would be a mistake.

## What TIA actually is

The Teacher Incentive Allotment started in 2019. It's the state's way of paying teachers more for being effective, not just for sticking around.

Districts opt in and design their own system, which the state has to approve. Teachers are measured two ways: classroom observations by an administrator, and how much their students grow over the year, usually on tests given at the beginning and end. The strongest teachers earn a designation, and it lasts five years.

Starting this school year, there are four levels, roughly matched to how teachers rank statewide:

- **Acknowledged:** top 50%
- **Recognized:** top 33%
- **Exemplary:** top 20%
- **Master:** top 5%

The money is real. For 2026–27, a Master teacher's allotment ranges from $12,000 to $36,000 a year, depending on how rural and high-poverty the campus is. Teachers at the schools that need them most can earn the most. Around 42,000 teachers in more than 800 districts are part of it now.

And it seems to work. A Texas Tech study of the first districts found a positive effect on keeping teachers and a moderate boost in student achievement.

<aside class="note">a raise for being good at your job. in teaching. wild concept.</aside>

## My TIA story

My first year, I earned an Exemplary rating. I worked my butt off for that.

My second year, I had a different evaluator. I don't think she cared for me. That year I was getting my master's in reading and literacy so I could be a better teacher, and my students grew *more* than they had the year before. I still scored lower on my evaluations.

So on paper, I looked like a worse teacher. Based on one person's opinion of me during one fifty-minute class period. That is crap.

My third year, my numbers would have earned me a Master designation, but I moved districts. This year, if my kids grow the way I expect them to, I'll earn Master. In my district, that means about $18,000 more a year.

Dude. That's amazing.

## What TIA gets right

**It rewards the work.** I'm an overachiever. I try my best to teach my kids, and I'm constantly learning how to do better. I'm getting a doctorate, for crying out loud. TIA is the first thing in my career that has said, out loud and with money, that the extra effort counts.

**It uses data, and I like data.** Most teachers hate that TIA is tied to testing. Maybe I'm different, but I like the information I get from exams. Tests tell me whether my instruction is working and how to adjust. Growth from the beginning of the year to the end is one of the fairest measures we have, because it doesn't punish a teacher for where her students started.

## What TIA gets wrong

**The evaluations are biased.** I lived it. Different evaluators will score you completely differently, and when a big piece of your pay rides on one person watching one class, that's a problem. My students' growth went up. My score went down. Those two things shouldn't be able to happen at the same time.

If Hinojosa's team wants to fix something, fix that.

## Why scrapping it is the wrong answer

What makes me mad about getting rid of TIA is simple: not all teachers are created equal.

I love my job. I'm constantly learning to be better at it. I genuinely want my students to grow. And I have worked alongside teachers who do the bare minimum, or less. I've seen teachers put in fake grades. I've seen teachers who only perform during a five-minute walk-through. Those are bad teachers.

How is it fair that they get paid the same as someone who goes above and beyond? Where is the incentive to be better and do better?

I want to believe that all teachers deserve a raise. Honestly, they don't. There are great teachers. There are bad teachers. There are burned-out teachers, and there are teachers who just don't care anymore. A system that pays all of them the same is telling the great ones that the extra effort doesn't matter.

Hinojosa isn't against paying teachers more. She said base salary is "just too low," that teachers "are not paid their worth right now," and that Texas "should have amongst the highest paid in the nation." I agree with all of that. Raise base pay. Please.

But she didn't put a number on it. She said, "We'll see what we have available." And I guarantee the state is not about to hand every teacher an $18,000 raise. So if TIA goes away and gets replaced with an across-the-board raise, I lose money. So does every teacher who earned a designation the hard way.

<aside class="note">"we'll see" is not a salary.</aside>

## Fix it. Don't scrap it.

Hinojosa says she wants to "respect" the teachers who have already earned TIA. The best way to respect them is to keep the program and fix what's broken: make the evaluations fairer, so one person's opinion can't sink a teacher whose students are thriving.

Raise base pay for everyone. And keep paying the teachers who go above and beyond. Those aren't competing ideas. Texas can do both.
