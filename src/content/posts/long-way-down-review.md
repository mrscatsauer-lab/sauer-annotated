I love this book. I'm just going to say that up front.

*Long Way Down* by Jason Reynolds is a novel in verse about fifteen-year-old Will. His older brother, Shawn, has just been shot and killed. Will knows the rules he grew up with: don't cry, don't snitch, and if someone you love gets killed, you get the person who did it. So he takes Shawn's gun, gets on the elevator, and heads down.

The whole story happens on that elevator ride. It lasts about a minute.

<aside class="note">one elevator. one minute. 300-some pages. it shouldn't work. it does.</aside>

## One floor at a time

The elevator stops at each floor on the way down, and at each stop, someone gets on. Someone from Will's past. Someone who's already dead. Each one has a piece of the story Will doesn't know, and each one makes him think harder about what he's about to do.

The sections are named for the floors, so the book is built for the classroom. We studied it floor by floor. Every floor gave us a new character to analyze, a new piece of the plot, and a new question about whether Will was going to go through with it.

My students were hooked the entire time. They came to class anxiously waiting for the next floor. At the end of the year, when everyone is counting down the days, that's saying something.

## Figurative language everywhere

Because it's written in verse, almost every page is doing something with language. Metaphor, simile, imagery, repetition, symbolism, all of it, and it never feels like a vocabulary worksheet. It feels like a fifteen-year-old trying to explain the worst day of his life. If you need examples of figurative language that students will actually care about, this book is full of them.

And it's a quick read. The verse moves fast and there's a lot of white space on the page.

<aside class="note">a "300-page book" that reads like 100. don't tell them.</aside>

## What we tied in

I taught it to my freshmen at the end of the year, and it covered a lot of ground in a short time:

**Characterization.** Every person who gets on the elevator is a character study. Who are they, what do they want from Will, and how does each one change what he believes?

**Plot.** The whole plot happens in about sixty seconds, which makes it a perfect way to talk about how authors build tension, use flashback, and pace a story. The real action is all in the past. The present is just a boy and an elevator.

## The ending is where it gets good

I won't spoil it, but the ending doesn't hand you an answer. My students had to decide for themselves what happened, and they did not agree.

Some were convinced Will might be dead. Some said he didn't go through with it. Others were sure he did. And they debated it, with evidence from the text, which is every English teacher's dream.

So we turned it into a Socratic seminar. Once they'd argued about what happened, they started arguing about what was right. Was Will wrong to want revenge? Were the rules he grew up with wrong? Who has the right to take revenge, if anyone does? Freshmen, debating justice and morality, using a book they actually read. There are so many good elements to argue in this one.

<aside class="note">the seminar questions basically wrote themselves.</aside>

## A heads-up for teachers and parents

This book deals with heavy topics: gun violence, the death of a brother, grief, and the pull of revenge. It's honest about all of it. That honesty is exactly why it matters, and exactly why you need to be ready for the conversations it starts.

Know your students. Read it first. Let families know what's coming.

## The verdict

Five stars. It hooked my students from start to finish, it's loaded with figurative language, it teaches characterization and plot without feeling like a lesson, and it gives you real things to talk about. And the ending gave my freshmen one of the best Socratic seminars I've had: what happened, what's right, and who gets to decide.
