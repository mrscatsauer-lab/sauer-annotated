Some stories make students cry. Some make them bored. "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" makes them *mad*.

My students were angry. Most of them were furious with the people who stay in Omelas. A few were just as furious with the people who walk away. Nobody was neutral, and nobody wanted to stop talking about it.

<mark>That anger is the lesson. All you have to do is aim it.</mark>

<aside class="note">a room full of honors kids yelling about ethics, with evidence. this is the dream.</aside>

Le Guin's story is only a few pages long, it won the Hugo Award in 1974, and it still starts arguments in every room it walks into. Here's how I taught it, built around imagery, argument, and a fishbowl discussion.

## The story in sixty seconds

Omelas is a beautiful city by the sea, celebrating its summer festival. There's music, horse races, bells, and kids running everywhere. Everyone is genuinely happy. The narrator keeps inviting *you* to add whatever would make the city perfect for you.

Then the narrator shows you the basement. In a small, dark room under one of the beautiful buildings, there's a child: alone, scared, filthy, and neglected. Everyone in Omelas knows the child is there. And the deal is simple: the happiness of the whole city depends on that one child's misery. If anyone helps the child, even once, everything good about Omelas disappears.

Most people learn the truth, struggle with it, and stay. A few walk out of the city alone, at night, toward a place the narrator can't describe. Nobody knows where they go.

<aside class="note">the story never tells you what to think. that's why it works. and why they're so mad.</aside>

## A note on one passage

Before you teach this, read it carefully yourself. Early in the story, while the narrator is inviting the reader to imagine whatever would make the city perfect, there's a short passage with adult content, including nudity and an orgy.

I chose to cut that passage for my students. I told both students and parents ahead of time what I removed and why. Nothing important is lost: the point of that section is that the narrator lets you build your own paradise, and the rest of the story makes that point clearly.

Being upfront mattered. Students knew exactly what was edited, and nobody felt like something was being hidden from them. Parents appreciated the heads-up. Check your district's guidelines and make the call that's right for your students, but whatever you decide, say it out loud.

## The lesson

This works over two or three class periods, depending on how long you let the fishbowl run. (Let it run.)

### 1. Read and track the imagery

Read the story together, aloud if you can. Le Guin's language is gorgeous, and the shift when you hit the basement lands harder when students hear it.

As they read, students keep a two-column chart:

| The festival | The basement room |
| --- | --- |
| What do you see, hear, smell, feel? | What do you see, hear, smell, feel? |

Then look at the two columns side by side. The festival is all light, color, music, and open space. The room is darkness, dirt, smell, and silence. Ask students: why does Le Guin describe the room in so much more specific, physical detail than the city? (Short answer: the city is a daydream. The room is real.)

<aside class="note">also notice how vague the place the walkers go is. she makes paradise and escape blurry on purpose. only the suffering is in focus.</aside>

### 2. Pick a side

Put three signs around the room: **Stay**, **Walk Away**, and **Something Else**. Students go stand under the one that matches what they'd do, then write a quick claim with one piece of evidence from the text.

The "Something Else" corner is where it gets interesting. Students who want to free the child have to deal with the story's rule: helping the child destroys everyone's happiness. Is that still the right thing to do? Some of them will say yes, immediately. Some will hesitate. Watch that hesitation. That's real thinking.

### 3. The fishbowl

Set up an inner circle of six to eight chairs, plus one empty "hot seat," and put everyone else in an outer circle.

- **Inner circle:** discusses. No hand-raising. They talk to each other, not to you.
- **Hot seat:** anyone in the outer circle can tap in, sit in the empty chair, make one point, and tap back out.
- **Outer circle:** tracks the discussion on the fishbowl tracker (at the bottom of this post). They write down the strongest point, a point they disagree with, and evidence someone used well.
- **Halfway through:** switch. The outer circle moves in, and the trackers become the talkers.

Same rule as any seminar: <mark>every claim has to point back to the text.</mark> "That's just wrong" doesn't count. "On the last page, the narrator says the walkers seem to know where they're going, so maybe..." counts.

<aside class="note">you will want to jump in. don't. sit outside the circle and take notes. they'll get there.</aside>

## Fishbowl questions

These are written for honors-level thinkers. You won't use all of them. Start with the opening questions and let the kids take it from there.

### Opening

1. Before the narrator showed you the basement, did Omelas seem real to you? What did you add to make it perfect?
2. When did you realize something was wrong? What word or detail tipped you off?

### Imagery and craft

3. Compare the festival imagery to the imagery of the basement room. Which description is more specific? Why would Le Guin make that choice?
4. The narrator keeps talking directly to "you." What does that do to you as a reader? Are you just reading about Omelas, or are you in it?
5. Why do you think the story never tells us where the walkers go?

### Argument: the choice

6. Is it wrong to stay in Omelas? Can a person be good and still stay?
7. The ones who walk away don't help the child either. Is walking away brave, or is it just a way to keep your own hands clean?
8. Should someone free the child, even if it destroys everyone else's happiness? Who gets to make that decision?
9. Is one person's suffering ever worth everyone else's happiness? What if it were a thousand people's happiness? A million?
10. Is there a choice the story doesn't offer? Why do you think Le Guin left it out?

### The real world

11. Where are the "basements" in our world? What comforts in your own life might depend on someone else's suffering?
12. Does knowing about that suffering make you responsible for it? What would walking away look like in real life?

### Closing

13. Would you walk away? If you would, where would you go?
14. Did anyone in the fishbowl change your mind, or make you less sure? Who, and what did they say?

<aside class="note">question 7 is the grenade. the kids angry at the walkers will finally get their turn.</aside>

## Wrap it up

End with a short written argument: *Who made the better choice, the ones who stay or the ones who walk away? Use at least two pieces of evidence from the story, and address the other side.*

Then, if you want a perfect companion, pair it with Ray Bradbury's "[There Will Come Soft Rains](/posts/there-will-come-soft-rains-review/)." One story is about a perfect world where everyone pays. The other is about a perfect world where one child pays for everyone. Students will see the connection on their own, and they'll love feeling clever about it.

> Omelas doesn't ask whether you'd walk away. It asks why you're still reading.
