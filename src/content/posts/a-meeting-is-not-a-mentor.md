On my first day as a teacher, I cried after second period. I walked straight out of my classroom at a Title I school, found my instructional coach, and told her I had no idea what I'd gotten myself into.

Y'all, I'm not talking about my twenties. I'm talking about four years ago. I was 38.

<aside class="note">a grown woman, hiding in the coach's office. character development.</aside>

She talked me down and told me to give it a week. It turns out I love teaching. But I would not be the teacher I am today without her, or without the daily PLC she ran for my first two years.

## What that coach actually did

She was honest with me, which is rarer than you'd think. When something wasn't working, she said so, and then she helped me fix it. She talked me down from anxiety more than once, and she always seemed to know the difference between a bad lesson and a bad career choice.

She also recorded some of my classes and watched them with me. If you've never watched yourself teach on video, I don't recommend it, and also you should absolutely do it. It's humbling. It's also how I got better fastest.

And our PLC was about data, every single day. I learned how to read student data, what it was actually telling me, and how to use it to plan what came next. I still do that every week.

<aside class="note">she doesn't get the credit anymore. so: hi. thank you.</aside>

## Then I changed campuses

Now I'm at a campus where PLC is few and far between. Most of us are lone rangers, the only teacher of our subject in the building, so there's nobody down the hall teaching the same lesson I am.

I'm okay with that. Four years in, I don't need a meeting every day. I know how to read my data, and I usually know when a lesson flopped and what to try next. I still love sitting with other teachers to listen and steal their good ideas. I just don't need it daily anymore.

But I think about first-year me a lot. If she had landed on this campus, alone, with no coach and no PLC, she'd be a completely different teacher. Maybe not a teacher at all.

That's my hill: PLCs are great, but they only work with the right timing, the right structure, and a coach who knows what she's doing.

## What the research says

Part of my doctoral coursework was reviewing a study on exactly this. Catheleen M. Benedict's 2014 dissertation at Rutgers followed one fourth-grade team as it tried to turn a messy PLC into a useful one.

The starting point will sound familiar. The meetings had no real structure. They didn't always look at student data, and when they did, nobody connected the numbers to their own teaching. Conversations wandered off.

<aside class="note">"wandered off" is academic for "we talked about the parking lot for twenty minutes."</aside>

Benedict added structure, and things shifted slowly. Trust had to come first, because nobody wanted to admit a lesson didn't work in front of colleagues. Then clear protocols for talking about data cut down on the chatter. Eventually the teachers started looking at their own practice, and they found a real, shared problem: their students struggled with higher-level vocabulary. They didn't just notice the low scores. They asked what they were doing that might be causing them.

That's the part that stuck with me. Data alone doesn't improve instruction. Somebody has to steer the conversation from "what are the scores?" to "what are we doing that led to these scores?" Usually that's a coach.

It's worth saying the study has limits. It was one small team at one school, the researcher was part of the group, and it didn't show measurable achievement data. It doesn't prove structured PLCs raise test scores everywhere. It just lines up with everything I've seen. The structured PLC, with an administrator and a coach in the room, made me better. The unstructured ones mostly made me late for lunch.

## What I'd tell a principal

New teachers need PLC every day. Their first two years are survival plus a crash course in everything nobody taught them in college. Veterans need it less often, but when it happens, it should be about something.

It also needs structure: protocols for looking at data, clear expectations, and a room where it's safe to say "my lesson bombed." And someone in that room has to actually know what they're doing. Someone who can read the data, ask the hard question, watch a recorded lesson, and tell a crying 38-year-old to give it a week.

For campuses full of lone rangers like mine, get creative. Pair teachers across grade levels or subjects. A monthly PLC built around literacy would work for almost everyone in the building, since every subject involves reading.

I think about how close I came to walking out after second period. One honest person and two years of daily support turned that into a career I love.

So if you're a brand-new teacher reading this after a rough second period: give it a week. Then go find your coach.
