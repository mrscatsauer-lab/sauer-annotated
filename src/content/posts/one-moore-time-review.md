First, a confession: I will read anything Mariana Zapata writes. Grocery lists. Terms and conditions. If she published her tax return as a slow-burn romance, I'd preorder it.

So it pains me a little to say this: *One Moore Time* is a slow burn, and in a few places it's just slow.

<aside class="note">this is my first review from my own nightstand. no lesson plan, no rubric. just me and my opinions.</aside>

## If you're new to Zapata

Mariana Zapata is famous for one thing above all: the slowest slow burn in romance. Her characters don't fall in love in a chapter. They fall in love over hundreds of pages of small moments, inside jokes, grudging respect, and one accidental hand brush that you will reread four times.

When it works, it's the best payoff in the genre. You've waited so long that every tiny step forward feels enormous.

<mark>The whole deal with a Zapata book is that the waiting is the point.</mark>

## The setup

Veronica Rubio is a real estate agent hired to sell the house of George Moore, a former champion swimmer with more gold medals than anyone. He's struggling. She's supposed to sell the house, collect a very nice commission, and move on. She can't quite make herself leave him to fend for himself.

That's all you get from me. No spoilers on my nightstand.

## Where this one dragged

The beginning. I got bored, which almost never happens to me with her books.

My problem was the real estate talk. Veronica sells houses for a living, so it makes sense that there's a lot of it, but early on I found myself skimming. In a Zapata book, the slow parts usually work because they're doing character work: you're learning who these people are through what they do and say. Here, some of the early stretches felt more like reading listings than reading a love story.

<aside class="note">i love a slow burn. i do not love a slow escrow.</aside>

## Also, I didn't like him

Here's the bigger problem: I didn't like Moore for a long time. Like, half the book.

He's immature, and Veronica spends a lot of the early chapters treating him like a child. Some of that is the point. He's a man whose whole identity was winning, and now he doesn't know who he is without it. But knowing *why* someone acts like an overgrown kid doesn't make me want to root for them to fall in love.

<aside class="note">i wanted to take away his phone and send him to his room.</aside>

That's tough in a romance, because the entire book depends on you wanting these two together. It's hard to get swept up in a slow burn when you'd rather the heroine just sell the house and go home.

To be fair, he does change. I saw the growth, and by the back half, he's someone who's earned a little of my affection. Once the relationship takes center stage, the book starts feeling like the Zapata I know. I just spent a lot of pages waiting for him to grow up first.

> A slow burn only works if you want them together. I spent half this book wanting him to grow up first.

## The verdict

Three and a half stars. If this were anyone else, I might have put it down in the first few chapters. Because it's Zapata, I trusted her, and she mostly paid that trust back. Moore's growth is real, it's just a long time coming.

**Read it if:** you're already a Mariana Zapata fan, you love a slow burn that makes you earn it, or you're patient with a slow start.

**Skip it if:** this would be your first Zapata. Start with one of her older books instead. This isn't the one that'll make you a fan.

**Pair it with:** a cozy blanket, a free weekend, and the willingness to push through the first act. It's worth it. Mostly.
