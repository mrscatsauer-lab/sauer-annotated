<mark class="clue">Boring.</mark> Every day was the same, and I liked it that way. Same bus, same red front door, <mark class="clue">same blue sticky note from Dad with a terrible history joke.</mark> Same peanut butter and bananas on my favorite red plate. Some kids hate routines. Routines are my favorite blanket.

That day, Mom wasn't home. Her note said she was dropping off cookies and Nanna would be by at 4:30. I was eleven now, old enough to stay home alone. I didn't feel old enough.

<aside class="note">a telescope he worked for. hold onto that.</aside>

On TV, Dead-Wrong Doug the weatherman promised thunderstorms, which meant no stars tonight and, worse, my telescope was still out in the tree house. <mark class="clue">I had worked two whole summers digging holes for Nanna to buy that telescope.</mark> I grabbed my red umbrella and opened the patio door.

<aside class="note">one eye shut. not a wink.</aside>

A huge green toad sat on the step, staring at me with one yellow eye. <mark class="clue">The other eye was squeezed shut, like it was winking. Or plotting.</mark>

"Ribbit," it said, in a voice <mark class="clue">way too deep for a toad.</mark>

<aside class="note">nanna is always right. remember that too.</aside>

I remembered Nanna saying <mark class="clue">frogs can be allies</mark>, poked the umbrella in its direction, and ran. I slipped, scraped my knee, grabbed my telescope, and locked the door behind me. When I looked back, the toad was gone.

Then I heard scratching at the front window. Then the phone rang.

"I'm watching you," said a deep voice.

I was already rocking on the kitchen floor when the front door banged open and my big sister, Lillee, came in laughing with her phone in her hand. "Got you, dork."

<aside class="note">he was wrong. who else has a deep voice?</aside>

She never explained the scratching. I figured she did that too. <mark class="clue">I was wrong.</mark>

Nanna arrived with pie ingredients and a story, like always. This one was about crab fairies who gather lightning glass from the beach and <mark class="clue">grind it into pearl dust that lets them fly.</mark>

"Nanna," I said, "fairies aren't real. Animals don't talk."

<aside class="note">file this one away. someone important says it again.</aside>

She cracked an egg one-handed. <mark class="clue">"How do you know? Have you ever heard one try?"</mark>

Her stories were so good I always checked with Mom or Dad, who'd shake their heads: not real. That was our secret code.

Then Dad burst in, soaking wet, holding a letter and a telescope made of solid gold. "Mom's been taken," he said, and read:

> *Captain Puffypants: Long ago you took something of mine, so now I have something of yours. Lady Julie is safe on vacation in the Wilds. Cross Dead Man's Sea and collect three pieces of a map. Together they lead to what was stolen from the sky. Bring it to me, with my lost looking glass, within a fortnight. You have my word she will be returned safely. The Queen of the Wilds.*

<aside class="note">"what was stolen from the sky." the whole plot is hiding in one line.</aside>

"Who is Captain Puffypants?" I asked.

Dad's ears turned red. Lillee laughed, then stopped, because Dad wasn't laughing. Dad always laughs.

"Is Mom okay?" Lillee asked. Her voice cracked in the middle.

"Your father was born in Dead Man's Sea," Nanna said quietly, setting down her mixing spoon. "So was I. The tree fairies, the talking animals, the crab fairies? Those are stories from home. And where we come from, a promise is a magic bond. If the Queen gave her word, your mother is safe. But a fortnight is only two weeks."

I looked at Dad for the head shake. He didn't shake his head.

"When I was a teenager," Dad said, rubbing the back of his neck, "I may have borrowed the Queen's golden looking glass. And her ship."

"You stole from a queen?" Lillee said.

<aside class="note">a dad who borrows things. watch the ending.</aside>

<mark class="clue">"Borrowed," Dad said. "For thirty years."</mark>

I sat down on the kitchen floor and rocked. Mom's note was still on the counter in her loopy handwriting. My world felt like a closet where everything falls out the second you open the door.

He handed me the glass. Through it, the kitchen shimmered like the inside of a soap bubble, and a pirate with a silver tooth and a wooden leg was grinning at me.

"Stinky Feet Pete," Dad said. "Old friend. He'll help us."

"He only has one foot," I pointed out.

Dad and Pete both burst out laughing. I still don't know why. You can absolutely be Stinky Feet Pete with one foot. It just means that foot is working extra hard.

That night we sailed out on the Sea Witch, a ship so broken down it looked haunted. Through the looking glass, it was brand new. Dad made me lookout. When I turned the glass toward the sky, every star I'd ever read about was there at once, and I forgot to breathe.

Then I saw daylight in the middle of the black ocean, like a window. The waves rose, a wall of water knocked me flat, and I hugged the glass and squeezed my eyes shut.

When I opened them, the sky was cotton-candy pink. Dad had a full beard and puffy pants covered in lightning bolts. Lillee said she was glad her friends would never see this. Pete was solid and real now, and his boot smelled like farts after a can of beans.

<aside class="note">who's she? she'll be back.</aside>

<mark class="clue">Off the bow, a girl with bright teal hair waved at us from the waves, then flipped a green-and-silver tail and was gone.</mark> Dad waved back like it was the most normal thing in the world.

"The stars here are the same as at home," Dad told me. "We follow the North Star to the Wilds."

<aside class="note">the rule. everything in this story is about to test it.</aside>

"The tip of the Little Dipper's handle," I said, and my chest felt looser. <mark class="clue">The stars are always the same. That's the rule.</mark>

Then the toads came.

<figure class="story-art wide"><img src="/art/golden-looking-glass/wog-sets-sail.webp" alt="Wog the toad, in a pirate hat and eye patch, sails a log boat with a golden leaf for a sail." loading="lazy" /></figure>

Hundreds of them, sailing tiny log boats with sails made of golden leaves, wearing quivers of toothpick arrows. <mark class="clue">The leader wore an eye patch.</mark> Dad threw open a chest of salt-water launchers. "They hate getting drenched. And whatever you do, don't let them lick you!"

<aside class="note">there's the eye. not winking after all.</aside>

It rained toads. Thump, thump, thump on the deck. Lillee and I blasted them off, two for every one that landed, until my hose died and five toads closed in. I dropped to the deck and curled into a ball.

"GET AWAY FROM MY BROTHER!"

<aside class="note">four words. they're coming back.</aside>

Lillee soaked every toad around me, and me too, then hauled me up by my arm. <mark class="clue">"I've got you, Gunth."</mark>

We fought side by side until the toads croaked, "Retreat!" Only then did I notice the burning. A tongue had caught my arm, and my skin was turning green and warty, the green creeping toward my elbow.

The eye-patched toad landed on my arm before I could scream. He hopped up and down it, and the green faded.

<aside class="note">remember what a toad lick does. it matters later.</aside>

"Toad slime cures <mark class="clue">toad licks</mark>," Pete said. "Everybody knows that."

"Name's Wog," the toad croaked, in a deep, familiar voice.

"You were on my porch," I said. "You were the scratching!"

Wog looked embarrassed, which is hard for a toad. "A pirate named Greybeard paid my clan to stop you. Said your dad was coming to drain our pond. So I crossed over to see for myself. What I saw was a scared boy who went out in a thunderstorm anyway, to save the thing he loved. Boys like that don't steal ponds."

He rolled a glass bottle to my feet. Inside was a scrap of paper covered in dots and lines, with a tiny crab drawn in the corner.

"That's a connect-the-dots," Lillee said.

"It's stars," I said. "Cassiopeia."

That night I climbed to the crow's nest to find the North Star. I found the Little Dipper. I followed the handle to the tip.

Nothing. Just a dark, empty hole in the sky.

Every night after the tree house, Mom tucks me in and asks which star I said goodnight to. It's always the North Star. Always.

The stars are always the same. That was my rule. If the stars could change, anything could, and Mom could stay gone. I started rocking, right there in the crow's nest. Wog hopped onto my knee and sat there, cool and heavy, like a little green paperweight.

After a while, my brain started working again.

<aside class="note">this is real. go outside tonight and try it.</aside>

You don't actually need the North Star to find north. Find the two stars at the front of the Big Dipper's cup, Merak and Dubhe. They're called the Pointer Stars. Draw a line through them and go about five times as far, and you land right on the North Star. Sailors have done it for thousands of years.

I followed the line. It pointed straight at the empty spot.

"Dad!" I yelled. "Someone stole the North Star!"

Dad reread the letter. *What was stolen from the sky.* "Greybeard," Pete growled. "No North Star, no way to find the Wilds. Every ship stays lost, and he raids them."

"The Pointers still work," I said. "They point to where it's supposed to be."

Dad put his hands on my shoulders. "Then you're our navigator. Where to?"

I held up the map. "Somewhere with crabs."

"Glitter Beach," Pete said. "Two days east."

<aside class="note">half a step toward believing on his own.</aside>

Glitter Beach sparkled where the lightning struck. Through the glass, the crabs had dragonfly wings. Nanna's story was true, and <mark class="clue">this time I didn't need anyone to tell me.</mark>

Their queen wore a bottle-cap crown. "Since the star vanished, the fog comes every night," she said. "My fairies fly out and can't find their way home."

<figure class="story-art wide"><img src="/art/golden-looking-glass/crab-fairy-class.webp" alt="Gunthar draws the Big Dipper in the sand for three crab fairies with dragonfly wings, one wearing a bottle-cap crown, while Wog watches." loading="lazy" /></figure>

So I sat in the sand and taught a hundred crab fairies the Pointer Stars, drawing the Big Dipper with a stick until they could do it themselves. It was the best class I'd ever been in, and I was the teacher.

That's when the fog rolled in, and a longboat with grey sails slid out of it. A huge man stood at the front with a beard tucked into his belt. His pirates flung a net, and <mark class="clue">nine crab fairies were caught</mark>, buzzing against the ropes.

<aside class="note">nine. count them later.</aside>

"Teaching crabs to find north?" Greybeard sneered. "I was the Queen's navigator once. Now every captain lost in my fog pays me to lead him out. Why would I let crabs find their way for free?" He eyed the gold glass in my satchel. "I'll be seeing you, boy."

Pete's cannon blasted salt water from the Sea Witch, and the longboat vanished into the fog, net and all.

I looked down at Wog. "Nanna says frogs can be allies. Would your clan help us?"

"Toads hate bein' lied to," Wog croaked. He whispered something grumbly into a seagull's ear, and it flapped off into the dark. <mark class="clue">"Ask a toad proper, and he'll come."</mark>

<aside class="note">a toad's promise. it's a magic bond, remember.</aside>

The crab queen pressed two things into my hands. The second map piece, with the Big Dipper on it. And a glowing pouch of pearl dust. "One pinch, and you'll fly for a count of one hundred," she said. <mark class="clue">"But never pour out the whole pouch. Whatever gets all of it rises up and never comes down again."</mark>

<aside class="note">a warning? or instructions? the biggest clue in the story.</aside>

As we rowed back, the girl with the teal hair popped out of the water beside us.

"Merian!" Dad said, grinning. "Kids, she's the oldest friend I have in this sea."

"The Queen gave me this to keep safe until a true navigator came," Merian sang, and held up the last piece. The Little Dipper, and at the tip of the handle, where the North Star belonged, a tiny island labeled FOG ROCK.

I laid the three pieces side by side. Cassiopeia. The Big Dipper. The Little Dipper. Every way there is to find the North Star.

"It's not a treasure map," I said. "It's a lesson."

The fog at Fog Rock was like sailing through oatmeal. Even the looking glass showed nothing but grey above us.

I opened the pouch. My hands were shaking. I'm almost never sure about anything. But Mom was past this fog, and I was the only one on the ship who knew exactly where to look.

One pinch, and my feet left the deck.

I floated up through the cold grey, counting. At twenty-two my head broke through into clear sky. Big Dipper. Pointers. The line.

"LEFT! More! STOP! Straight ahead!"

"Aye aye, Navigator!" Dad called.

At ninety-one I sank back down, and the fog thinned around a black island and an ugly grey ship. At the top of its mast hung an iron cage holding a light so white it hurt to look at.

Greybeard's pirates swung over on ropes, and everything turned to chaos: Dad and Pete blasting water, and then, out of the fog behind us, hundreds of deep voices croaking at once. Wog's clan had come, just like he promised.

Then Lillee screamed.

Greybeard had her at the top of his mast, dangling her over the water. "The looking glass, boy, or your sister swims!"

Everyone looked at me. I thought about Lillee hauling me off the deck. *I've got you.*

I held up the glass. "It's yours!"

Greybeard reached down for it, and that was the second he stopped watching his feet.

<aside class="note">there's the lick. and there's the eye patch, all the way up the mast.</aside>

Wog, who had quietly hopped up the mast while everyone stared at me, landed on his face and gave him the sloppiest lick in the history of the Wilds.

Greybeard turned green, howled, and let go of Lillee.

She fell. I threw a pinch of pearl dust, and she stopped, and floated down like a dandelion seed, right into my arms.

<aside class="note">told you they'd come back.</aside>

"I've got you, Lillee," I said.

Greybeard splashed into the sea, surrounded by two hundred angry toads. "He'll be scrubbin' lily pads for a hundred years," Wog croaked happily.

Down in Greybeard's hold, Lillee found the net, all nine crab fairies still inside. She untangled every one of them herself, and they buzzed around her head like a crown.

Dad brought down the cage. The North Star was the size of a baseball, warm as cocoa, and humming like a happy cat. But when he opened the door, it didn't rise. It had been locked up too long.

*Whatever gets all of it rises up and never comes down again.*

That wasn't a warning. That was exactly what the star needed. I turned the pouch upside down and poured every grain over it.

<figure class="story-art tall"><img src="/art/golden-looking-glass/the-star-rises.webp" alt="Gunthar pours glowing pearl dust from a pouch as a smiling star rises from an open iron cage, with Wog cheering below." loading="lazy" /></figure>

It rose, slowly, then faster, a white streak through the thinning fog, until it settled at the very tip of the Little Dipper's handle. The fog melted like sugar in tea, and every star came out at once.

At the Isle of the Wilds, Mom was waiting on the beach in sunglasses, drinking out of a coconut. "What took you so long?" she laughed, hugging us. "The mermaids here give foot massages."

"You were kidnapped," Lillee said.

"Well. Yes. But very politely."

The Queen of the Wilds had silver hair and the look of a grandmother who could sink your ship if she felt like it. Dad knelt and returned the golden looking glass. "I'm sorry I borrowed it."

"For thirty years, Paul. I wrote you letters when Greybeard stole the star."

"I thought they were junk mail," Dad mumbled.

The Queen turned to me. "The crab fairies tell me who taught them to find north. And the North Star tells me who put it back."

"Stars can't talk," I said, before I could stop myself.

<aside class="note">there it is. nanna's line, from a queen.</aside>

The Queen smiled. "How do you know? Have you ever heard one try?"

I looked at Dad for the secret head shake. He just smiled.

The Queen held the glass out to me. "Keep it, Gunthar. The Wilds need a navigator."

I wanted it so badly my fingers itched. Then I thought about my own telescope, the one I dug all those holes for.

"Thank you," I said. "But I already have a telescope. I earned it." I pushed the glass back. "And you should keep yours this time. Somebody might borrow it."

The Queen laughed so hard a flock of fairies fell out of a tree.

<aside class="note">same routine. different boy.</aside>

The next Monday was boring again. Bus. Red door. Sticky note: *What's a pirate's favorite subject? ARRRRT. Love, Captain Puffypants.* Peanut butter and bananas. Dead-Wrong Doug promised clear skies, and for once he was right.

That night in the tree house, I found the Big Dipper, followed the Pointers, and there it was. The North Star, right where it belonged.

The ladder creaked. Lillee's head poked through the floor. No phone.

"Hey, Gunth. Show me how you find it?"

So I did.

And down in the grass, so quietly only someone with super-power hearing would notice, a deep voice croaked, "Ribbit."

I looked down. A toad sat in the moonlight, one eye covered with a tiny black patch. I waved. He winked.

<aside class="note">a real wink this time.</aside>

Some things change. Some things stay the same. And some things, like stars and sisters and toads who keep their word, are there even when you can't see them.
